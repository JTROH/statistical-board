What do “target effect”, “noise SD” and “power” mean, and where do I get the numbers?
Noise SD (standard deviation)
Run the same settings several times and the results will not be identical. The SD is the typical size of that scatter. It sets the smallest change that could be nothing but chance.
Where to get it: the centre points of a past study; repeat batches at one set-point; or 4–6 repeat runs now — then SD ≈ (highest − lowest) ÷ 2 for 4 repeats, ÷ 2.5 for 6. Two repeats are not enough: the guess is easily off by half.
Target effect
The smallest change in the response you would actually act on. Not the change you hope for — the smallest one that would matter. If a 0.3 g/L gain in titer would change a decision, that is the number. Same units as the response.
Signal-to-noise (target effect ÷ noise SD)
How big your signal is compared with the scatter. Above about 2 it is clear and small designs will do. Between 1 and 2 it needs a careful design. Below 1 the change is smaller than the noise and needs many runs. The line above the table shows yours as you type.
Power
The chance the study finds your target effect if it is really there. 80% power means a 1-in-5 chance of running everything and seeing nothing, even though the effect exists. It is not a pass mark; it is the odds you are accepting. More runs, a larger target effect, or less noise all raise it.
“Smallest change it can reliably see”
The same fact the other way round: the smallest real change a design would find at 80% power, in your units. If that is bigger than your target effect, the design is too small for your question. Each option also shows what happens to power if your noise SD guess is 1.5× too low, because the noise figure is the one people are least sure of. If the scatter grows with the level (titres, cell counts), choose CV %: give the noise as a CV and the target as a fold change (1.5 = +50%). Power is then worked out on the log10 scale, and the results must be analysed as log10 too.
